Case Presentation
A 23‐year‐old, right‐hand‐dominant woman presented to the orthopedic outpatient department with swelling of the left forearm of 9 months' duration and pain in the same region for the preceding 4 months. The swelling had been noticed incidentally and had enlarged gradually to approximately half the size of a tennis ball, without any antecedent trauma and without overlying skin changes. Over the last 4 months, the swelling had become painful. The pain was throbbing and intermittent in nature, nonradiating, aggravated by physical activity, and relieved by oral analgesics. There were no constitutional symptoms such as fever, night sweats, anorexia, or weight loss and no history of similar swellings elsewhere in the body.

Her past medical and surgical history was unremarkable, with no known comorbid illness and no long‐term medication. Family history, including any history of skeletal or neoplastic disease, was noncontributory and her psychosocial history was unremarkable. She had received no prior intervention for the swelling other than intermittent self‐administered analgesics, which provided only temporary relief.

On general physical examination, the patient was comfortable and afebrile, with no swelling in any other part of the body and an otherwise unremarkable systemic examination. On local musculoskeletal examination, there was a single oval swelling measuring approximately 8 × 3 cm over the anterior aspect of the left mid‐forearm (Figure 1A). The overlying skin was normal in color and temperature, with no redness, dilated veins, or ulceration, and was freely mobile over the swelling. The swelling was firm in consistency, well defined, nonmobile, and fixed to the underlying structures. Mild tenderness was elicited on deep palpation. No bruit was audible on auscultation over the swelling. Elbow, forearm, and wrist movements were preserved. The regional lymph nodes were not palpable, and the distal neurovascular status of the limb was intact.

All baseline laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, serum calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, and renal and liver function tests, were within normal limits.

Plain radiographs of the left forearm in anteroposterior and lateral projections demonstrated an eccentric ballooning expansion of the mid radial shaft with radiolucency and septate loculations, associated with cortical thinning (Figure 1B). Contrast‐enhanced MRI of the left forearm showed a well‐defined lobulated lesion of abnormal signal intensity with multiple internal fluid–fluid levels in the anterior (volar) compartment of the mid forearm, measuring 3.5 × 3.7 × 6 cm (anteroposterior × transverse × craniocaudal), with associated cortical erosion involving the mid radial diaphysis (Figure 1C). The soft tissue component partially encircled the bone with mild extension into the posterior compartment and anteriorly displaced the forearm muscles. The lesion showed heterogeneous enhancement with a central nonenhancing area, thick fascial enhancement extending from the tumor margins, producing a tail sign, and peritumoral edema encasing the interosseous vessels with displacement of the median nerve and radial vessels. The reported radiological differential diagnoses were soft tissue sarcoma with telangiectatic change, telangiectatic osteosarcoma, and subperiosteal aneurysmal bone cyst.

Three‐phase skeletal scintigraphy performed with technetium‐99m methylene diphosphonate showed increased blood flow and blood pool accumulation of tracer over the left forearm, with a focal area of increased radiotracer uptake over the mid shaft of the left radius on delayed images; the remainder of the axial and appendicular skeleton showed symmetrical uptake. The findings indicated active bone pathology of the mid radial shaft suggestive of a primary bone tumor requiring histopathological correlation. Contrast‐enhanced CT of the chest and whole abdomen, performed as part of the staging work‐up, showed no evidence of pleuropulmonary metastasis; a few axillary lymph nodes with preserved fatty hila and small hypodense hepatic lesions with marginal vascularity, consistent with hemangiomas, were noted as incidental findings.

For definitive characterization, an incisional biopsy was performed. Microscopic examination revealed fragments of a neoplastic lesion comprising abundant osteoclast‐like multinucleated giant cells between which spindle‐shaped cells were interspersed. The individual cells had moderate cytoplasm and round to oval nuclei with variably prominent nucleoli, and mitotic activity of up to 3 per 10 high‐power fields was appreciated. Areas of hemorrhage with cystic change were also present. Immunohistochemistry for H3F3A G34W (H3G34W) was negative, and no malignant osteoid was identified. The features were reported as consistent with aneurysmal bone cyst in correlation with the radiological findings. Because a limited incisional sample cannot entirely exclude telangiectatic osteosarcoma or a secondary aneurysmal bone cyst arising in another lesion, the definitive diagnosis was reserved for examination of the complete resected specimen (Section 2.6). The principal diagnostic challenge in this case was the nonspecific nature of the imaging findings. Multiple fluid–fluid levels, cortical breach, a soft tissue component, and peritumoral edema are all features shared by aneurysmal bone cyst and telangiectatic osteosarcoma, and the initial radiological report favored a malignant etiology. The differential diagnosis therefore included telangiectatic osteosarcoma, soft tissue sarcoma with secondary bone involvement, giant cell tumor of bone, unicameral bone cyst, and fibrous dysplasia. Sequential investigation was required, with imaging, skeletal scintigraphy, staging CT, and finally tissue diagnosis obtained at separate visits, which contributed to an interval of several weeks between presentation and definitive surgery. Giant cell tumor of bone was considered unlikely on the combined clinical, radiological, and histological picture rather than on immunohistochemistry alone: the lesion was centered in the diaphysis without epiphyseal or subarticular extension, it contained multiple fluid–fluid levels with hemorrhagic cystic change, and the osteoclast‐like giant cells were admixed with spindle cells rather than evenly distributed within sheets of mononuclear cells, as is characteristic of giant cell tumor. The negative H3G34W immunostain was supportive; however, because a small proportion of giant cell tumors harbor H3F3A mutations other than G34W and are negative with this antibody, the result was interpreted only in conjunction with the morphological and radiological findings and was not regarded as excluding giant cell tumor in isolation. Telangiectatic osteosarcoma was excluded on examination of the entire resected specimen, which showed no malignant osteoid, no marked nuclear pleomorphism, and no atypical mitotic figures, and the final diagnosis was a solid variant of aneurysmal bone cyst of the radial diaphysis.

Intralesional curettage with bone grafting was initially considered, but was not undertaken because of the size of the lesion, the extent of cortical destruction of the diaphysis, the presence of a soft tissue component abutting the neurovascular bundle, and the radiological possibility of a telangiectatic osteosarcoma. Although the incisional biopsy favored aneurysmal bone cyst, several considerations led the treating team to prefer en bloc resection. First, the lesion was large, with cortical erosion of the mid‐radial diaphysis and an extraosseous soft tissue component that partially encircled the bone; curettage would have left a thin, structurally incompetent cortical shell at high risk of pathological fracture and would still have required structural grafting and internal fixation. Second, the soft tissue component was closely related to the median nerve and the radial and interosseous vessels, making thorough intralesional clearance and the safe use of local adjuvants (high‐speed burring, phenol, or cryotherapy) difficult without risk to these structures. Third, recurrence after intralesional treatment is more frequent in large, aggressive lesions, and recurrent disease adjacent to the neurovascular bundle would have made any subsequent resection more hazardous [4]. Fourth, the imaging had raised concern for telangiectatic osteosarcoma, and the biopsy represented only a limited sample; en bloc resection with a cuff of normal tissue therefore provided definitive treatment and complete histological assessment without compromising margins had a malignant diagnosis emerged. Finally, the diaphyseal location allowed resection without sacrificing the elbow or wrist articulations, leaving an intercalary defect amenable to biological reconstruction. En bloc resection was therefore selected as the definitive procedure in order to obtain wide margins and minimize the risk of local recurrence.

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia with the patient supine, the affected limb on an arm table, and an arm tourniquet applied. Perioperative intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis was administered at induction. The lesion was exposed through a modified Henry approach to the volar forearm. The lesion, which measured approximately 10 × 6 cm in situ, including its extraosseous soft tissue component and the surrounding reactive tissue, was identified (Figure 2A). The radial artery and the median nerve were identified and protected throughout the dissection, and the affected segment was carefully isolated and removed en bloc with a cuff of surrounding normal tissue (Figure 2B). The resected specimen was sent for histopathological examination.

Following resection, a segmental bone defect of approximately 11 cm remained (see below for the relationship between lesion size and defect length). Reconstruction was performed using a nonvascularized fibular strut autograft (Figure 2C), which was contoured to the defect, seated within the proximal and distal radial stumps, and stabilized with a locking compression plate (Figure 2D). Intraoperative imaging confirmed satisfactory alignment and fixation. The tourniquet was released, hemostasis secured, and the wound closed in layers over a suction drain. The limb was immobilized in an above‐elbow slab.

Histopathological examination of the entire resected specimen demonstrated a lesion measuring 7 × 3 × 1.5 cm composed predominantly of solid cellular areas of bland spindle‐shaped fibroblastic cells with scattered osteoclast‐like multinucleated giant cells, reactive woven bone, and foci of hemorrhage, with only a minor component of blood‐filled cystic spaces separated by fibrous septa. The predominance of these solid areas over the classic cystic architecture formed the basis for the designation of a solid variant of aneurysmal bone cyst. There was no marked nuclear pleomorphism, no atypical mitotic figures, and no malignant osteoid, and no background lesion suggesting a secondary aneurysmal bone cyst was identified. All soft tissue and bone margins were uninvolved.

The different dimensions reported for the lesion reflect different methods and stages of measurement. The clinical dimension (8 × 3 cm) was a surface estimate of the palpable swelling, including the overlying soft tissue. The MRI dimensions (3.5 × 3.7 × 6 cm) describe the well‐defined lesion itself, with approximately 6 cm of craniocaudal extent along the radius. The intraoperative estimate (approximately 10 × 6 cm) was made in situ before resection and included the extraosseous soft tissue component, the reactive pseudocapsule, and surrounding edematous tissue. The pathological dimension (7 × 3 × 1.5 cm) was measured on the formalin‐fixed specimen, after drainage of the blood‐filled spaces and fixation‐related shrinkage. The length of the bone defect (approximately 11 cm) was determined by the resection levels rather than by lesion size alone: the osteotomies were placed beyond the longitudinal extent of abnormal signal on MRI to obtain a margin of normal bone, because telangiectatic osteosarcoma had not been excluded preoperatively, and the radial ends were then freshened to healthy, bleeding cortical bone to permit stable graft–host contact. The pathological bone margins (1.0 cm proximally and 0.8 cm distally) were measured on the fixed specimen and are therefore expected to be smaller than the corresponding in vivo distances.

The postoperative course was uncomplicated. The wound healed primarily and the sutures were removed at 2 weeks. The limb was immobilized for 6 weeks, after which radiographs were obtained and a graded physiotherapy program comprising active and active‐assisted elbow, forearm, and wrist mobilization followed by progressive strengthening was commenced. The patient adhered fully to the immobilization protocol and the physiotherapy program and attended all scheduled follow‐up visits at 2 weeks, 6 weeks, 3 months, and 6 months. No adverse or unanticipated events occurred; in particular, there was no surgical site infection, no postoperative neurovascular deficit, no implant failure, and no donor site morbidity.

Serial radiographs demonstrated progressive incorporation of the strut graft. At 6 months of follow‐up, the patient reported no pain and had returned to her routine daily activities. Goniometric range of motion of the operated left upper limb was 145° of elbow flexion and 0° of extension, and 77° of wrist flexion and 70° of wrist extension, all within normal limits, and 60° of forearm pronation and 65° of supination, giving a rotation arc of 125° with a residual deficit in both pronation and supination. The Musculoskeletal Tumor Society (MSTS) upper‐extremity score was 27/30 (90%) [6] and the QuickDASH score was 18 [7], indicating a good functional outcome with mild residual disability. Radiographs at that visit demonstrated complete union at both graft–host junctions with maintained alignment and intact fixation, and no radiological evidence of local recurrence (Figure 3).

The patient reported that she had initially ignored the swelling because it was painless, and that she sought medical advice only when the pain began to interfere with her daily activities. She described considerable anxiety during the diagnostic period, when the possibility of a malignant tumor was raised and several investigations were required before a diagnosis could be reached. She found the explanation of the surgical plan and of the reasons for removing a segment of bone reassuring, and stated that being told the final biopsy showed a benign lesion was the most important moment in her treatment. She reported that the period of immobilization was inconvenient but tolerable, and that she was satisfied with the outcome, as she is now free of pain, able to use her left arm normally for household and occupational tasks, and comfortable with the appearance of the scar.
