Case presentation
A 39-year-old man, a former footballer, presented with pelvic pain of approximately two years' duration. The pain began insidiously, was sharp and non-radiating. It was intermittent, was aggravated by strenuous activity such as playing a football match and was relieved by over-the-counter analgesics. He denied any direct trauma preceding the onset of pain. The symptoms had made playing football progressively less enjoyable, which prompted consultation at our orthopaedic surgery department, where he was assessed and managed throughout. There was no unintended weight loss, night sweats, persistent fever or unrelenting fatigue. He reported regular alcohol consumption, did not use tobacco, and was on no routine medications. He reported no family history of a similar presentation and no personal or family history of previous ectopic bone formation.

On examination he was conscious, oriented and appeared well, with vital signs within normal limits; the conjunctivae were pink and the sclerae anicteric. Bilateral symmetric swellings were present in both groins, with bony-hard palpable masses that were tender on palpation and not fixed to the overlying skin. The range of motion of both hip joints, including abduction and adduction, was full, symmetric and painless, and the distal neurovascular status of both lower limbs was intact. There was no local warmth, erythema or overlying skin change. The remainder of the examination was unremarkable.

An anteroposterior radiograph of the pelvis demonstrated bilateral, well-defined, horn-like ossific lesions projecting from the medial aspects of the superior pubic rami (Figure 1). Three-dimensional CT reconstruction confirmed symmetric, exuberant ossification within the adductor musculature along the anterior aspect of both pubic bones (Figure 2). On independent review, the reporting radiologist described the appearances as symmetric exuberant HO of the adductor musculature along the anterior aspect of both pubic bones, considered definitely benign, with no evidence of osteochondroma, and favoured sequelae of old injury or trauma.

The preoperative working diagnosis was HO, with osteochondroma, osteoma and chondroma as the main differential diagnoses. Osteochondroma was considered because each lesion projected from the surface of the pubis, but the lesions were bilateral and symmetric, and on CT neither showed continuity with the pubic medullary cavity, the defining feature of an osteochondroma. Osteoma was unlikely because osteomas consist of dense ivory bone and arise mostly in the craniofacial skeleton, whereas these lesions were mature trabecular bone at a muscle origin. Chondroma was unlikely in the absence of chondroid matrix mineralisation or cortical scalloping on imaging. Because of persistent activity-limiting pain and diagnostic uncertainty, excisional biopsy was undertaken after the patient gave informed consent. The surgery was done three months following the initial consultation. Through an anterior approach to the pelvis, both lesions were exposed and excised. Intraoperatively, each mass was continuous with the cortex of the pubic bone through a broad bony base, without a discernible cleavage plane, requiring osteotomy at the base to achieve excision; the surrounding adductor musculature was otherwise unremarkable (Figure 3). The adductor origins overlying each lesion were mostly elevated from the mass with a periosteal elevator, although a small portion of the insertion was partially detached during exposure; the remainder of the insertion was left in continuity with the pubic cortex. The osteotomy was deliberately performed outside the tendinous footprint, so that no tendon required formal detachment or reattachment. The excised fragments measured up to approximately 7.5 × 2.5 cm.

Histopathological examination showed fragments of benign bone with fatty marrow; rare fragments contained portions of hyaline cartilage, and there was no evidence of malignancy (Figure 4). These histological elements are not specific, and identical fragments may be seen in osteochondroma. The diagnosis therefore rested on radiologic-pathologic correlation. Three findings excluded osteochondroma. The ossification was bilateral and symmetric and lay along the adductor musculature rather than forming a solitary stalked exostosis. The lesions showed no continuity with the pubic medullary cavity. The cartilage in the specimen was scant and fragmentary, with no cartilage cap showing organised endochondral maturation. Taken with the absence of malignancy, these findings established the diagnosis of mature HO at the adductor origin of the pubic bones.

The postoperative course was uneventful and the patient was discharged home on a one-week course of an NSAID (diclofenac) for analgesia. He was allowed to bear weight as tolerated from the first postoperative day, with no restriction of hip movement. He was seen at six weeks with only minor pain and needed no rehabilitation. At follow-up one year and four months after surgery he was well, with no pain and no palpable mass, and he had resumed sporting activity, although not at a professional level. Follow-up was clinical; no surveillance radiographs were obtained.

From the patient's perspective, he was very satisfied with the outcome of surgery. He reported that the resolution of his pain and, above all, his ability to resume his work were the most important gains for him, and he was pleased to have returned to playing recreational football.
