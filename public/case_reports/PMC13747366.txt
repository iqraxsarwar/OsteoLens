Case presentation
An active 54-year-old man presented to our hospital with a 1-year history of chronic left ankle pain. The pain (VAS score = 6) developed gradually and progressively worsened, particularly during weight-bearing and daily activities. During this period, the patient managed his symptoms at home with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs without seeking medical care. The patient denied any history of trauma, smoking, infection, allergies, or prior ankle surgery and had no known chronic illnesses or relevant comorbidities.

On physical examination, the ankle showed no erythema, warmth, or swelling. Palpable crepitus was elicited during ankle motion, accompanied by a restricted range of motion compared with the contralateral side. No joint instability or neurovascular deficit was identified.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and inflammatory markers, were within normal limits, effectively excluding inflammatory or infectious etiologies. Plain radiographs of the left ankle revealed multiple well-defined intra-articular loose bodies and an associated anterior tibial spur (Figs 1A and B). Subsequent computed tomography (CT) scan and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of multiple loose bodies within the ankle joint, predominantly involving the anterior and lateral compartments, without evidence of aggressive features or malignancy (Figs 1C–F). The differential diagnosis included anterior ankle impingement, osteoarthritis with loose-body formation, and synovial chondrosarcoma. Anterior ankle impingement was considered because of the anterior tibial spur and restricted range of motion, while osteoarthritis was considered because of the presence of intra-articular loose bodies. However, CT and MRI demonstrated multiple well-defined loose bodies without aggressive imaging features, making SC more likely. Figure 1.Plain radiograph of the left ankle in (A) AP view and (B) lateral view showing multiple intra-articular loose bodies (red arrow) and an anterior tibial spur. Computed tomography in (C) sagittal and (D) axial planes, and T2-weighted MRI in (E) sagittal and (F) axial planes of the left ankle, showing the presence of multiple intra-articular loose bodies (red arrow).

Given the persistent symptoms and mechanical limitations, the patient underwent ankle arthroscopy under spinal anesthesia using standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals. Arthroscopy revealed multiple cartilaginous loose bodies in the anterior and lateral gutters with hypertrophic synovium (Figs 2A,B). Complete loose-body removal, synovectomy, and anterior tibial spur resection were performed (Figs 2C,D). Histopathological examination of the excised loose bodies confirmed SC with no evidence of malignant transformation (Figs 3A,B). Figure 2.Intraoperative arthroscopic findings: (A, B) Multiple cartilaginous loose bodies; (C) Arthroscopic removal of loose bodies; (D) Arthroscopic view following complete loose-body removal and anterior tibial spur resection. Figure 3.(A) Gross appearance of the excised intra-articular loose bodies. (B) Histopathological examination reveals cartilaginous nodules (hematoxylin and eosin staining, 100×). (C–E) Clinical appearance of the left ankle at 3 months postoperatively, demonstrating restored range of motion. (F, G) Follow-up radiograph of the left ankle at 3 months postoperatively shows no residual or recurrent loose bodies.

Postoperatively, the patient was allowed weight-bearing as tolerated starting on postoperative day 1, with early range-of-motion exercises encouraged. The postoperative course was uneventful. At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported complete resolution of ankle pain (VAS = 0) and had regained full, painless range of motion (Figs 3C–E). He had returned to normal daily activities without limitation. Follow-up radiographs demonstrated no residual or recurrent loose bodies within the ankle joint (Figs 3F,G). At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained free of any complaints. At the 9-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no clinical evidence of recurrence.
